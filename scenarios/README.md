# Practice Scenarios & Capstones

> **Three completed capstones from this folder will out-compete five certifications on a resume.** That is not marketing. It is what hiring managers tell me, off the record, every time we talk about junior hires.

This folder is the **doing** part of the roadmap. Modules 0 to 16 build the vocabulary and the mental models. Module 17 (and this folder) is where you prove you can use them.

## What lives here

- **The scenarios themselves** are in [`modules/17-practice-scenarios.md`](../modules/17-practice-scenarios.md). Ten realistic, ambiguous situations you would face in a real GRC, security, or privacy role. Each comes with stakeholders, constraints, deliverables, a rubric, and hints you should not peek at until you have attempted the work.
- **The templates** in [`templates/`](templates/) are the scaffolds you fill in to produce those deliverables: a risk register, a DPIA, a Statement of Applicability, an IR runbook, a vendor assessment, a policy template, and an audit evidence pack.

The scenarios tell you *what* to produce. The templates show you *what good looks like* when you produce it.

## Why this folder exists separately

A common mistake: people read Module 17, nod along, and never actually do a scenario. The deliverables sound like a lot of work. They are. That is the point.

The other common mistake: people produce deliverables that are technically correct but look nothing like real workplace artefacts. A risk register that is a bullet list is not a risk register. A DPIA that does not name a lawful basis is not a DPIA. The templates here exist so you produce something a real auditor or DPO would recognise.

## How to use this folder

**Pick a scenario from Module 17 that matches your target role.**

- Aiming for GRC analyst, privacy associate, or compliance roles? Start with Scenarios 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 9.
- Aiming for security engineer, security architect, or AppSec? Start with Scenarios 3, 7, 8, 10.
- Aiming to demonstrate breadth (career-shifters into senior roles)? Pick one from each cluster.

**Then:**

1. Read the scenario in full. Do not skim.
2. Identify which templates from [`templates/`](templates/) apply. Most scenarios use two or three.
3. Open a private GitHub repo (or a folder on your machine if you are not ready to publish). Copy the templates in. Rename them for the scenario.
4. Fill them in as if your job depended on the output. Time-box yourself. A real evidence scramble does not give you three weeks.
5. Write a 2-page README for the scenario, framing the problem, your approach, and what you would do differently with more time. The README is the most important file. It is what a hiring manager reads first.
6. Compare your output against the rubric in Module 17. Be honest.

**Do not peek at the hints in Module 17 until you have attempted the scenario.** The hints describe what strong answers cover and what weak ones miss. Reading them first is the same as reading the answer key before sitting the exam. You will feel clever and learn nothing.

## How to use these as portfolio

Once you have three scenarios you are not embarrassed by:

1. Sanitise. Remove anything that looks like real client data, even if you invented it. Use the recurring fictional EdTech SaaS from the modules ("LearnLark") or invent your own.
2. Make the repo public. Pin it to your GitHub profile.
3. Add it to LinkedIn under **Featured**. Use the word *capstone*, not *project*. *Project* sounds like coursework. *Capstone* sounds like work.
4. Bring two of them ready to discuss in interviews. Not recite. Discuss. Be ready to defend the trade-offs you made and name what you would do differently.

A senior interviewer will not test whether your DPIA is perfect. They will test whether you can talk about *why* you made the calls you made. That is the entire game.

## What good submissions look like

The signal a hiring manager looks for, in this order:

1. **You distinguished what you knew from what you assumed.** Real work is full of unknowns. Strong submissions name them explicitly.
2. **You prioritised.** You did not try to fix everything. You picked the two or three things that mattered and defended the choice.
3. **You communicated up.** The board memo, the email to the CTO, the customer-facing letter — these are graded harder than the technical artefacts. Most candidates are decent at the technical work and terrible at the communication. Be the candidate who is not.
4. **You wrote like a person.** Plain English. Short sentences. No jargon used to impress. If a non-technical executive cannot follow your exec summary, it is not an exec summary.

## A note on AI

You can use an AI assistant to help you draft. You should not use it to think for you. The whole point of these scenarios is to build judgement, and judgement does not transfer when someone (or something) else makes the calls.

A useful test: after you finish, close the laptop, walk away for an hour, come back, and explain your top three decisions out loud to an empty room. If you cannot defend them without re-reading what you wrote, you do not actually know your own work, and you will fail the interview discussion.
